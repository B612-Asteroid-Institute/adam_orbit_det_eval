**Can LOOO detect 1-second timing errors? TL;DR: No, not the way we've been trying — and the literature explains exactly why.**

We ran simulations across 100 MBAs (mean rate ~750 arcsec/day) and 100 NEOs (~1800 arcsec/day), injecting a 1-second timing error at each of 9 MPC observatories, then using our Leave-One-Observatory-Out pipeline to see if the held-out station's along-track (AT) residuals flagged the error. Result: 0 out of 9 detections for both populations. For comparison, a constant astrometric bias is detectable at >50σ and a trailing-length bias scores 9/9. So the pipeline works — timing is just much harder. The best corrected AT signal we saw was ~2.6σ at Spacewatch (703, 31K observations), which is nowhere near reliable detection. The core problem is that our per-observation timing SNR is roughly 0.03–0.07 (signal ~0.009–0.021 arcsec, noise ~0.3 arcsec), and you need about 10 million observations to reach 3σ — not 10–30 thousand.

The best published comparison is the IAWN fast-flyby campaigns. Farnocchia et al. (2022, PSJ) observed 2019 XS with ~950 observations from ~50 stations when it was screaming past at 3.8 arcsec/second at closest approach. They found a network-wide timing bias of −0.5 seconds — times reported to the MPC were about half a second early — and isolated one station (Y00) to −0.66 ± 0.05 s. A follow-up campaign on 2005 LW3 (Farnocchia et al. 2023, PSJ) confirmed the negative bias persists across the network. The reason those campaigns work is SNR: per-observation signal is roughly 6 arcsec vs. our 0.009–0.021 arcsec, a factor of 200–400× more signal per measurement. They also use an externally-anchored orbit (radar + previous observations) so the orbit fit doesn't absorb the bias — which is exactly the problem our LOOO approach runs into. When we refit the orbit on the held-out station's complement, the fit partially absorbs the timing signal, we estimate by ~40–50%.

There's also a subtler issue the literature points to. Carpino, Milani & Chesley (2003) showed that same-night observations from one station share the same timing error, so the effective sample size for detection is number of nights, not number of observations. That alone cuts our sensitivity by another factor of several. And for NEOs specifically, the RA timing signal tends to cancel: because NEOs are observed at all orbital phases, the east-west component of their motion averages close to zero across the full dataset, so the timing bias in RA washes out. The Stronati et al. (2023) paper on station-level AT/CT error stratification is consistent with undetected timing biases at individual stations but can't isolate them from other systematic effects.

What would actually work for 1-second timing detection across the MPC catalog? Three things stand out: (1) Rate-stratified AT analysis — plot mean AT residual vs. object rate of motion for each station; the slope of that line equals the timing offset Δt directly. This is essentially what IAWN does but applied to the full catalog. (2) Explicitly fit Δt as a free parameter in orbit determination alongside the orbital elements; this avoids the absorption problem entirely. (3) Prioritize close-approaching NEOs: even a handful of tracklets at >0.1 arcsec/s give 10× the per-observation signal of a typical MBA, so a few hundred such tracklets per station gets you to detection. The LOOO framework we've built is still genuinely useful for constant and trailing biases — we should keep it for those and treat timing as a separate problem requiring a different diagnostic.
