Observatory Astrometric Bias — Preliminary Findings

A Leave-One-Observatory-Out analysis of 3,500 numbered minor-planet orbits

Executive summary

We measured per-observatory astrometric bias by holding out each observatory in turn, refitting the orbit from the remaining data, and computing the residuals on the held-out observations. Across 246 observatories and roughly 1.08 million held-out observations drawn from 3,500 numbered minor planets, we find a substantial fraction of stations show systematic offsets in reported right ascension and/or declination that are larger than chance alone can explain.

Observatory bias overview

Each point is one observatory, plotted by its measured mean offset in right ascension (horizontal) and declination (vertical). Point size scales with the number of held-out observations contributing; color encodes how well the station's stated uncertainties match the scatter we actually observe.

How to read it. An observatory at the origin is unbiased on average. Distance from the origin is the magnitude of systematic offset in arcseconds. Cool-colored (blue) points are stations whose reported uncertainties agree with their actual scatter; warm-colored (red) points report overly optimistic uncertainties. Larger points dominate the global astrometric catalog; small points represent stations with few observations in this sample, and their positions carry wider confidence intervals. The most extreme outliers are almost all small-sample stations — their bias estimates are noisy and should not be over-interpreted until the full-scale run delivers more observations per station.

Well-calibrated observatories

High-volume stations whose mean bias is below 0.05″ in both axes and whose uncertainty model is within a factor of three of observed scatter. These are our reference-quality stations and set the noise floor for what an astrometric pipeline can achieve today.

Calibration quality distribution

χ²-per-observation measures how well each station's claimed astrometric uncertainty matches the scatter we actually see. A value of 1.0 is ideal calibration; values above 1.0 mean the station's error bars are under-reported; values much above 1 mean an astrometric pipeline that relies on those uncertainties will be misled.

Confidence and limitations

How to interpret these results.

  • 3,500 objects is a meaningful but small sample — roughly 1–2% of the numbered minor-planet catalog. Results are most reliable for stations that observed many of these objects and become progressively noisier for stations with only a handful of contributions.
  • The bootstrap 95% confidence intervals quantify statistical uncertainty from the finite sample only. They do not capture systematic modeling errors such as uncorrected non-gravitational forces on individual asteroids.
  • The LOOO method measures the net bias per station, which can combine multiple physical effects — star-catalog errors, timing offsets, differential atmospheric refraction, and image trailing — into a single number.
  • The full along-track / cross-track decomposition, which separates timing-like errors from geometric ones, is not yet populated in this preliminary pass. It will be available for the full-scale run.

What changes at full scale.

  • Scaling from ~3,500 to ~200,000 objects will shrink statistical confidence intervals by roughly a factor of eight. Bias estimates that are currently marginal (stations with under a few dozen contributing objects) become meaningful.
  • A program-code breakdown becomes feasible, which lets us track bias changes over time as observatories adopt new star catalogs or upgrade instrumentation.
  • Independent empirical uncertainty models per observatory, per reference catalog, can be derived and fed back into downstream orbit-determination pipelines.
Methodology note

For each object, we iterate over the observatories that observed it. We hold out all observations from one observatory, refit the object's orbit from the remaining astrometry using a full N-body propagator, then compute the predicted position at each held-out observation time and compare it to the reported position. The difference — the residual — is free of any influence from that observatory's own measurements. Aggregating residuals across many objects reveals the station's systematic bias.

Statistics are object-weighted: each object contributes equally to a station's mean bias regardless of how many observations it has at that station. This prevents a handful of high-cadence targets from dominating what should be a station-level statistic.

The sample is 3,500 numbered minor planets drawn deterministically (fixed seed) from the MPC catalog, with a minimum of 20 observations per object. An object is excluded from a station's statistics if holding out that station would leave fewer than six observations, fewer than seven days of arc, or more than 80% of observations held out. Orbit fits are performed by FindOrb differential correction; propagation uses ASSIST N-body with relativistic corrections.
